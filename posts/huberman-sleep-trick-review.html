An evidence review • 23 November 2026

Huberman’s sleep trick:
what holds up?

Real biology. An unproven shortcut to sleep.

A review of the supplied viral post, its proposed mechanism, and the related research.

Executive summary

The eye-movement routine in this post is unproven, rather than established neuroscience. Its explanation borrows from real connections between eye movements, balance and the brain, but those connections do not demonstrate a sleep-inducing switch. Slow eye movements during drowsiness are an observation, not proof that copying them makes you fall asleep. Small studies of rocking beds are interesting, but they test a different intervention. Slow breathing has better support for physiological calming, while evidence that it reliably improves objectively measured sleep remains mixed. Huberman does acknowledge indirect evidence in an interview, although he then describes the mechanism and results much more confidently. Trying a comfortable relaxation routine is reasonable; advertising it as a dependable neurological shortcut is not. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11

Legend

Accurate

Core claim backed by strong, convergent evidence.

Exaggerated

Based on real evidence, but makes stronger claims than the evidence supports.

Unproven

May be true, but lacks adequate direct evidence.

Incorrect

A central factual or quantitative claim conflicts with established evidence.

Each stamp judges the claim as written. “Unproven” does not mean “false.” This review focuses on the eye-movement post, not Huberman’s entire sleep toolkit. Background claims and reviewer interpretations are labeled separately.

Point by point · The central promise

01 The eye routine makes you fall asleep quickly

Unproven
Claim in the post

The suggested sequence is closed-eye movements from side to side, up and down, then circles, followed by a long exhale. A targeted search did not locate a published randomized human trial testing this exact sequence against a suitable comparison. That leaves its average benefit, success rate and effective “dose” unknown. Someone may find it soothing without the routine being a proven sleep treatment. 1, 4, 11

Why this rating: No direct trial was located; that is a limitation of the evidence search, not proof the method cannot work.

02 It switches off awareness of body position

Unproven
Explanation in the post and interview

The eyes, balance system, brainstem and cerebellum really are connected. That does not demonstrate that voluntary eye circles switch off the brain’s tracking of the limbs, or that doing so causes sleep. Establishing that explanation would require measuring both the proposed change and sleep, rather than inferring them from the anatomy. 3, 5

Why this rating: The relevant circuitry is real; the claimed chain of events has not been demonstrated by the evidence located.
Point by point • 02

The biology behind the story

03 You must forget your body position to sleep

Unproven
Related claim in the interview

Attention and awareness change as sleep develops, but that is different from a prerequisite you must deliberately create. Sleep onset involves many interacting processes, and its boundaries are surprisingly difficult to define. Treating loss of body awareness as an established switch gives a simple story more certainty than the evidence warrants. 3, 4

Why this rating: A possible accompanying experience is being promoted into a necessary mechanism; the cited sleep research does not establish that requirement.

04 Slow eye movements occur as people get sleepy

Accurate
Relevant background—not a separate claim in the screenshot

A 2023 review of 139 studies describes slow eye movements increasing around the transition into light sleep, then declining as sleep deepens. The review covers sleep onset broadly; it is not 139 trials of this trick. Observing an eye pattern during drowsiness does not show that deliberately copying it produces drowsiness. 4

Why this rating: The observation is well established; reversing the direction of cause and effect remains an inference.

05 Rocking research validates the eye trick

Exaggerated
Related rationale—not a trial of the screenshot’s routine

A 2019 crossover study of 18 adults found that a continuously rocking bed shortened entry into non-REM sleep and improved some sleep measures. A different 2018 study in 18 men did not find faster sleep onset, though some measures changed during rocking. Neither tested voluntary eye movements. Moving the body stimulates the balance organs; moving the eyes alone is not the same intervention. 5, 6, 7

Why this rating: There is real human research behind rocking, but transferring its benefits to eye circles is an unsupported leap.

06 Slow breathing can help the body settle

Accurate
Evidence relevant to the post’s long exhale

A 2024 meta-analysis of 31 studies found short-term changes in heart rate and heart-rate variability with slow breathing; effects on reported stress were less clear. A 2023 randomized study also found mood and breathing-rate benefits from repeated exhale-focused practice. These findings support a modest relaxation rationale, not a guaranteed response to one breath. 8, 9

Why this rating: Converging evidence supports physiological calming; changing a body measurement is not itself proof of falling asleep.

What was actually tested?

Rocking-bed experimentsEffects of moving the body in particular settings.Equivalent effects from moving only the eyes.
Breathing studiesEffects of sustained breathing practices on specified outcomes.That the full eye routine works—or why.
Personal success storiesWhat someone experienced after trying it.What would have happened without it.

This distinction matters more than whether an explanation includes impressive-sounding brain anatomy.

Point by point • 03

Breathing, anecdotes and certainty

07 A longer exhale is the special ingredient

Unproven
Possible interpretation of the advice—not explicitly claimed in the post

An exhale-focused routine can help without a longer exhale being uniquely necessary. In a 12-week trial of 100 adults, longer-exhale and equal-length inhale/exhale slow breathing did not differ significantly in stress reduction. That does not prove the patterns are identical, but it argues against treating one precise ratio as established magic. 10

Why this rating: The head-to-head evidence does not establish superiority, and this trial measured stress rather than speed of falling asleep.

08 The breathing step reliably speeds sleep

Unproven
Implied benefit of the combined routine

A 2026 review included nine bedtime slow-breathing studies with 457 participants. Self-reported sleep generally improved, but findings from movement monitors and sleep-lab recordings were inconclusive. Many studies using objective measurements lasted only one night, while several self-report studies lasted about a month. This is promising evidence for a practice, not proof that one long exhale promptly induces sleep. 11

Why this rating: Subjective benefits deserve attention, but direct sleep measurements and the exact proposed protocol remain uncertain.

09 Success stories establish that the trick works

Unproven
Evidence offered by the post

People may honestly report falling asleep after trying it. Without a comparison, we cannot separate the eye movements from ordinary sleepiness, the breathing, reduced worry, expectations, or natural night-to-night variation. Remembering only the next morning also does not reveal exactly how long falling asleep took. These are alternative explanations, not accusations that anyone invented their experience. 1

Why this rating: An experience can be real while its cause remains unknown; testimonials cannot estimate a reliable treatment effect.

10 “Neuroscience says it works”

Exaggerated
The viral post’s headline

The post combines genuine sleep and breathing science with an untested eye routine, then presents the package as established. Huberman’s interview includes the qualifier “indirect scientific support,” although his subsequent explanation is substantially more confident. The fair description is an experimental relaxation idea with an unproven mechanism—not a scientific breakthrough, and not a disproven fraud. 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11

Why this rating: The confidence of the headline exceeds the direct evidence; neither enthusiasm nor skepticism substitutes for a controlled test.
What would strengthen the case? A randomized study comparing the full routine with breathing alone and a credible control, using measured time to sleep across multiple nights. A separate test of body-position awareness would be needed to support the proposed explanation.
Conclusion

A relaxation experiment, not a sleep switch

Valid takeaway

A quiet, comfortable routine may help you unwind, and slow breathing has a more credible evidence base than the specific eye sequence. Nothing needs to be purchased. If you try it, keep the movements and breathing gentle, stop if uncomfortable or dizzy, and drop it if it becomes frustrating. There is no research-backed reason to strain, force an extreme gaze, or perform the sequence perfectly.

For persistent insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has substantially stronger support. It addresses habits and thoughts that sustain sleeplessness; it is more than a list of sleep-hygiene tips. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I, while recommending relaxation alone only conditionally. 12, 13

Things to be skeptical of

Watch for three substitutions: a body measurement standing in for actual sleep, an experiment on one intervention being used to endorse another, and a personal success story becoming a universal explanation. None proves the trick useless. They explain why “it might help me relax” is a reasonable conclusion and “it shuts down a specific circuit” is not yet justified.

Huberman and his methods

Huberman is a Stanford neuroscientist with genuine expertise in vision and neural circuits; he also coauthored the breathing trial discussed here. 9, 14 That makes his ideas worth examining, not automatically clinically validated. In this example, my assessment is that his initial qualification is appropriate but is undermined by the certainty of the explanation that follows. The viral post removes the caveat altogether, so responsibility for the overstatement is not confined to either the researcher or the repost. We cannot establish his intentions from this material.
